# JLPT N3 Grammar: The Patterns That Decide the Test

N3 is the great filter of the JLPT — the first level where reading passages stop being notices and become articles, and where grammar stops being one pattern per question and starts being several nested in one sentence. The jump from N4 is real. Here's what actually distinguishes people who pass.

## The jump from N4 in one sentence

At N4 you learned *forms*. At N3 the test asks whether you understand **how forms connect to meaning** — nominalizers, clause-linkers, and particles that mark the *role* of a phrase inside a longer sentence.

## The nominalizer family (こと vs の vs わけ)

N3's most frequent grammar trap is the difference between こと, の and わけ — three ways to make a verb act like a noun:

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **〜こと** | the abstract fact / the act itself | 日本語を教える**こと**が好きです（I like teaching Japanese） |
| **〜の** | the concrete event / the thing observed | 彼が歌って**いるの**を見た（I saw him singing） |
| **〜わけ** | the reason / the logical conclusion | そういう**わけ**で遅れました（That's why I was late） |
| **〜わけがない** | there's no way that's true | 本当だという**わけがない**（There's no way it's true） |

The exam runs this pair constantly in reading passages — わけ in particular carries the *tone* (reasoning, conclusion, or rebuttal) and is often the word the reading question hinges on.

## The stance markers (として / に対して / にとって / によると)

These four particles look similar and do very different jobs:

| Form | Marks | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **として** | the role/identity under which something happens | 小説家**として**有名になった（became famous as a novelist） |
| **に対して** | the person/thing the action is directed at | 経験豊富な人**に対して**頼みたい |
| **にとって** | the perspective from which a judgment is made | 私**にとって**大切なこと |
| **によると** | the source of information | 天気予報**によると**雨だそうだ |

The test distinguishes them by *what's after*: identity (として) → a state; a target person (に対して) → an action; a viewpoint (にとって) → a value judgment; a source (によると) → information.

## The cause family (おかげで / せいで / ばかりに / くせに)

N3 loves the politeness of causes — whether the speaker credits or blames:

- **おかげで** — thanks to (positive result): 毎日ジョギングを続けている**おかげで**、体が軽くなった
- **せいで** — because of (negative/blame): 寝坊した**せいで**遅刻しました
- **ばかりに** — just because (unintended bad outcome): 一言足りなかった**ばかりに**誤解された
- **くせに** — despite (criticism of a claim): 弱い**くせに**強がる

Same cause-effect structure, four emotional registers. The reading question usually hinges on which register the passage is using — because that tells you how the author feels.

## The conditionals at N3 (ば・たら・なら・と・ばかり)

Every condition still shows up — but N3 tests the *nuance*:

- **と** — automatic/natural consequence: 春に**なると**桜が咲く
- **ば** — general condition, often for advice: 時間が**あれば**来てください
- **たら** — one-time future condition: 日本に**着いたら**連絡してください
- **なら** — if it's the case that (topic-setter): あさって**なら**大丈夫だよ
- **ばかり** — just/only just: 日本に来た**ばかり**です

なら is the most N3-tell: it responds to a suggestion rather than laying a condition. In the exam, the choice between なら and たら in a dialogue usually separates passers from near-misses.

## The appearance family (そうだ / ようだ / らしい / ように思われる)

Four ways to report what something seems like — the difference is *source of information*:

| Form | Source | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **〜そうだ** (from looks) | what the speaker visually perceives | 赤くなっている → 食べてもよさ**そうだ** |
| **〜そうらしい** | what others say | あのお店はおいしい**らしい** |
| **〜ようだ** | reasoned inference from evidence | 電気がついていない → 留守**のようです** |
| **〜ように思われる** | hedged personal judgment | そのうわさは本当ではない**ように思われる** |

Note the difference between そうだ (*appearance*) and ようだ (*inference from evidence*) — the exam builds reading questions on that distinction.

## The aspectual pair (ている vs てある vs ておく)

The three different states a て-form can hold:

- **〜ている** — ongoing state: ドアが開いて**いる**（it's open — someone opened it）
- **〜てある** — completed on purpose, result visible: ドアが開けて**ある**（someone deliberately opened it and left it like that）
- **〜ておく** — prepared in advance (for a later need): 買い物に行く前に食べ物を買って**おく**

The difference between ている and てある is whether the state was intentional — and that distinction decides multiple reading questions.

## The sentence-assembly test at N3

The hardest N3 question type remains the same as N4's but harder: order four fragments, and pick which goes in the marked slot. What changes at N3 is that the **fragments themselves are longer** (clause-sized), and grammatical clues hide inside them (て-forms, particles, verb stems). The tactic is unchanged: translate each fragment, identify the head and tail, then the slot lands one of the middle fragments.

## Practice in context, not from lists

Every pattern above exists in dozens of books. What N3 actually rewards is recognizing them **inside a real sentence with the particles and conjugations already in place** — which is what practice tests train.
