# How to Study Kanji: A Method That Actually Holds to Test Day

Most kanji advice is either "just memorize them" or a 40-minute system complicated enough that you quit on day three. Here's the middle version — a method that holds because it's built around what memory does, not around ideal study.

## Step 1: Meet radicals before kanji

A kanji without its components is a picture; a kanji with its components is a word you can decode. Learn **~50 radicals** first (人, 水, 木, 手, 心, 言, 貝…), then every new kanji becomes a combination of things you already know.

The payoff is geometric: 650 kanji becomes roughly 300 "radical families + variations" — about half the total memory load.

| Radical | Family of kanji it unlocks |
|---|---|
| 氵（water） | 川・流・波・海・湖・酒 |
| 言（speak） | 話・語・読・説・訓 |
| 貝（shell = money） | 買・売・貸・貨・費 |
| 食（eat） | 飲・飼・飯・餐 |
| 亻（person） | 休・体・作・住・借 |
| 日（sun/day） | 明・間・時・昨・映 |
| 手（hand） | 打・持・投・抱・折 |

You don't need to learn these as separate study items — just meet each one once, then every new kanji that reuses it is a *modified* memory rather than a new one.

## Step 2: Learn the kanji with a word, never alone

The fastest way to forget a kanji is to learn it as a shape. A kanji is only usable inside a **word** — which is also how the JLPT tests it (inside furigana-tagged sentences, not as isolated symbols).

For every new kanji, learn **one word with it** as a fixed pair:

| Learn this | Not this |
|---|---|
| 買う (かう, "to buy") + 買物 (かいもの, "shopping") | 買 → "buy" (shape only) |
| 貸す (かす, "to lend") + 貸して (かして) | 貸 → "lend" (shape only) |
| 沸騰 (ふっとう, "boiling") | 沸 → "boil" (unattached) |

That word-pair habit is why spaced-repetition decks that show compound readings work — the shape, reading and meaning travel together.

## Step 3: Reading vs. writing — pick your priority

The JLPT does **not** test handwriting. It tests recognition: given 貸す or 貸して, do you know what it means and how it's read?

So **reading ability is the target** — not stroke order. Practically:

- **Learn to READ first** — the shape, the reading, the word. This is what pays on the exam.
- **Skip writing practice** unless you enjoy it for its own sake — it triples study time for a skill the test doesn't measure.
- **Exceptions:** if you're preparing for a job in Japan or Japanese school entrance, add writing later; for JLPT, spend that hour on reading input instead.

## Step 4: Space it (SRS — no way around this one)

Cramming works for 1–2 weeks. After a month, you've forgotten the majority of what you crammed. Spaced repetition schedules each review at the moment you'd otherwise forget:

| Review # | Typical interval | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| First | same day (within hours) | Consolidates the fresh memory |
| Second | 1–2 days | Pulls it out of "short-term" |
| Third | 3–6 days | Moves it toward long-term |
| Fourth | 1–2 weeks | Hardens it |
| Fifth | 3–6 weeks | Locks it in |
| Then | months | Maintenance only |

The principle isn't the intervals — it's that **every review happens just as you're about to forget**. Reviewing too early wastes time; too late means relearning.

## Step 5: The mistake loop (your highest-value cards)

Generic kanji lists tell you what *someone else* found hard. Your own wrong answers tell you what *you* find hard:

- Every JLPT question you get wrong shows exactly which kanji in context beat you — and how (reading misread? similar-looking kanji confused? word read as another with the same shape?)
- Flashcards built from your mistakes carry **that context with them** — which sentence tripped you, which distractor fooled you
- Over a few weeks, your "mistake-deck" concentrates on the 20–30 kanji that are genuinely costing you points, instead of the 30 you already know

That's the loop: **test → mistake → card → spaced review → test**. It's faster and more targeted than any generic list, because your personal failure points are visible in the data.

## The two real blockers (and how to handle them)

1. **Similar-looking kanji** (貸/貨/資/質, 待/侍, 未/末). The fix isn't more cards — it's **contrast pairs**: learn 貸す (lend) next to 貨 (freight) and 質 (quality/substance). The difference becomes a story ("the one with 貝 is about money").
2. **Readings that change by context** (日 = にち/ひ, 人数 = にんずう). The fix isn't memorizing readings in isolation — it's memorizing the **word** (人数 = people count) and letting the reading ride along inside it.

## What a week of this looks like

| Daily slot | Activity | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Morning (anchor) | Flashcards: 10 new + review of due cards | 15–20 min |
| Commute/idle | Reading input: 2 short JLPT-style passages | 10 min |
| Evening | Mistake review from today's practice questions | 10 min |

That's about 35–40 minutes of kanji work per day, which is enough to reach N5's 100 kanji in 2–3 weeks and N4's 300 in about 2.5 months — at sustainable pace, without burnouts.
