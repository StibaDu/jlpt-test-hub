# Japanese Language Schools in Tokyo: How to Pick an Affordable One

Tokyo has hundreds of Japanese language schools — from intensive 5-day/week boot camps to 2-evenings-per-week conversation clubs. Prices range from ¥2,000 for a single event to nearly a million yen for a year with a visa. The difference between a good and a bad choice is rarely the teaching quality — it's whether the school's structure matches your goal and your timeline.

Here's how to choose without overpaying.

## School types at a glance

| Type | Schedule | Cost ballpark | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intensive language school** | Mon–Fri, 3–5 h/day | ¥800,000+/year (visa included) | Serious N5→N1 sprint + student visa |
| **Part-time school** | 2–5 evenings/week | ¥15,000–50,000/month | Beginners staying long-term |
| **Language café** | Flexible, pay-per-event | ¥2,000–4,000/event | Conversation practice, networking |
| **JLPT prep courses** | Weekend intensives | ¥30,000–80,000/level | Quick exam boost |
| **Culture/specialized courses** | 2–8 week blocks | ¥80,000–250,000/course | Learning + doing (manga, anime, business) |

A useful rule of thumb: the more hours per week, the lower the cost *per hour* — but the tighter your daily schedule. An intensive school costs roughly ¥1,500–2,000 per lesson hour; a pay-per-event café can be ¥3,000+ for the same hour, but you only pay it when your schedule allows.

## Why "cheap" and "expensive" are misleading labels

A ¥900,000/year intensive school with a student visa is often *the affordable option* — because it buys you:

- A **student visa** that legally allows up to 28 hours of part-time work per week
- Access to the cheapest housing tier (student share houses and dorms)
- A visible daily structure, which is the single biggest predictor of progress

Meanwhile a "cheap" ¥15,000/month part-time course gives you no visa rights and no structure. If you're job-hunting, sightseeing and studying in the same week, part-time study frequently dies by the third week — not because the school is bad, but because nothing forces you to show up.

So frame the question differently: **what do you need the school to force you to do?** If you need structure and a visa, go intensive. If you need flexibility because you already work or study full-time, go part-time and accept that discipline is now your job.

## Affordable pick in Tokyo: ALA

[**ALA (アメリカ語学センター/アラ)**](https://ala-school.jp) — after checking many schools in Tokyo, this is one of the more affordable options with solid group courses and a central location. Schools in this price band usually charge ¥150,000–250,000 for a 3-month intensive term instead of the ¥250,000+ in central Shibuya/Shinjuku — a difference of ~¥400,000 per year, which in Tokyo is your rent for three to four months.

## Use a placement agency — free for you

Agents like [Go! Go! Nihon](https://www.gogonihon.com/en/?ref=jlpttesthub) match you with schools, handle the paperwork and visa steps, and cost nothing — the school pays their commission. Worth doing for the visa support alone: they check your documents before the school does, and a rejected visa application costs you months.

What agencies actually handle:

1. School selection matched to your level, budget and city
2. Application forms and payment to the school
3. **Certificate of Eligibility (CoE)** application at Japanese Immigration — the step where most errors happen
4. Visa application at the embassy in your country (after the CoE arrives)
5. Optional housing and airport pickup

## What to check before enrolling

1. **Class size** — 8 students max is ideal; 20+ means little speaking time
2. **Textbook system** — ask which book; Minna no Nihongo and Genki are standard, trustworthy names
3. **JLPT track record** — do they publish pass rates?
4. **Visa support** — only relevant if you need one (school-sponsored student visa requires 6+ months of study)
5. **Level placement** — can you enter mid-level, or only at term start?
6. **Term rhythm, not just hours** — many intensive schools use 3-month terms with 2-week breaks. Ask when terms start; if the next one is in 8 weeks, that changes your arrival date entirely
7. **Refund policy** — if you fail your CoE or visa, is the deposit refundable?
8. **Where the students come from** — a school with 90% students who share your native language pushes everyone into their own language within a week. A mixed classroom forces Japanese.

## The visa math, concretely

A student visa through a language school unlocks **up to 28 hours/week** of part-time work. Tokyo's typical hourly pay (konbini, restaurants, warehouses) is ¥1,100–1,400. At 20 hours/week and ¥1,200, that's roughly ¥100,000/month gross — often close to a working student's full cost of living in a share house.

That changes the "expensive school" calculation completely: the school is partly an investment in your ability to work legally at real hours. On a tourist/working-hour-limited status, you can't legally work at all.

## What a school will not fix

Language schools in Tokyo are good at teaching Japanese and bad at two things:

1. **Test format drills.** Teachers optimize for fluent, correct Japanese — the JLPT rewards recognizing and eliminating wrong answers fast. These are related skills, not identical ones.
2. **Kanji volume.** N5 needs ~100 kanji, N4 ~300, N3 ~650. No 3-hour class transfers that; it happens on flashcards and reading practice at home.

That's why the strongest results come from combining a school with self-study on the format itself — which is exactly what practice tests measure.
