Working Holiday Jobs in Japan: Types, Wages & Where to Find Them

If you're on a working holiday visa (or a student visa with permission to work), Japan offers a surprisingly wide range of part-time jobs — most paying about ¥890–1,500/hour (roughly €7.40–12.40).

Before the job list, the three rules that matter more than any individual employer:

  1. 28 hours/week is a hard ceiling — and it's counted combined across all jobs. Two jobs at 14 hours each is allowed; 18 + 12 is not.
  2. Your visa paperwork travels with you — keep the work-permission stamp (資格外活動許可, shikagai katsudō kyoka) accessible; employers ask for it at hire.
  3. Payday cycles matter. Japanese part-time jobs commonly pay on the 15th and/or the end of the month, and your first paycheck can be 30–45 days after your first shift. Bring startup money for the first two months.

💡 You can work up to 28 hours per week — but if you have two jobs, the combined total still counts: 14 hours each.

What jobs can you do?

  • Restaurants, cafés and fast-food chains — the classic starter job
  • (English-language) kindergartens and day-care centers
  • Nanny & childcare
  • Shop assistant — if your language skills are sufficient
  • Language teacher — not just at formal schools: language cafés and conversation clubs (also for French, Spanish, German, Chinese...)
  • Photo model for advertising and performer for TV
  • Farm work
  • Hotels, ryokans, ski resorts
  • Light factory work, packaging goods
  • Moving companies

Higher Japanese level or a professional qualification → higher hourly wages.

Jobs by Japanese level

Not every job needs Japanese at the same level — and this is the fastest way to plan your first weeks:

Your level Realistic jobs Typical hourly pay
Beginner (JLFT N5 in study) Dishwashing/kitchen prep, fruit picking, warehouse packing, moving company, Uber Eats ¥1,000–1,300
Basic conversation (N5 passed) Convenience store shelf-stocking, clothing chains (WEGO), hotel cleaning, ski-resort general staff ¥1,000–1,300
Confident conversation (N4) Convenience store counter, café/restaurant floor, ryokan reception, farm customer-facing work ¥1,100–1,400
Polite forms (N3+N) Izakaya staff, shop assistant in bigger chains, guest relations at hotels ¥1,200–1,500
Native/teaching language Language café conversation staff, private lessons, kindergarten English ¥1,500–3,000

The pattern: the physical, low-language jobs pay similar to the service jobs — but the service jobs pay off double later, because they build fluency you can sell again. Kitchen prep for six months gives you savings; cashier work for six months gives you savings and conversation.

Where job seekers actually find work

LanCul (ランカル英会話)

Language cafés that hire English/German/Spanish speakers as conversation staff.

"Private teaching is a great way to earn extra money. You can find students by attending networking events and meetups. LanCul frequently hosts parties where you can meet Japanese people interested in private lessons."

World Unite!

Organized placements: farm work, ryokan and hotel jobs, ski resorts.

Uber Eats (Tokyo)

Delivery work on your own schedule — signup directly. No interview, no Japanese test, immediate start. Pay is per delivery (¥300–600 in central Tokyo), so effective hourly rates land around ¥1,000–1,600 after vehicle costs — good for flexibility, weaker for learning Japanese.

Ask in person: WEGO and clothing shops

WEGO (fashion chain with stores all over Tokyo) — you can ask for work directly at the store. People with limited Japanese have found part-time work there successfully.

The two channels people ignore

  1. Job boards in Japanese: Yoriso (youriso), WeWork.jp, Baitoru (バイトル) and Tsutaetai list thousands of daily part-time openings. Filters matter: search 外国人歓迎 ("foreigners welcome") and 1日4時間以内OK ("under 4 hours OK").
  2. Job fairs for foreigners — Tokyo runs recurring events (Hello Work's foreign-worker desk, plus university/embassy-organized fairs). Bring your CV in Japanese AND English; the same CV in both languages reliably doubles interest.

What the "keigo wall" actually costs you

Every hospitality job interview includes the same test: can you say the standard greeting with the right politeness level? Fail it and the shop politely passes — not because you can't do the work, but because the shop's customers will hear you all day.

The phrases that decide interviews in practice:

  • いらっしゃいませ（ Welcome）— with the correct pitch, not mumbled
  • ありがとうございました・恐れ入りますが
  • 少々お待ちくださいませ
  • すぐお伺いいたします
  • いかがいたしましょうか

That vocabulary overlaps almost entirely with the "Höflichkeit & Ausdruck" category of the JLPT — which is why practice tests double as job preparation.

The Japanese you'll need on the job

Even izakaya work starts with keigo (polite forms) and basic customer phrases — exactly what the JLPT N5–N4 tests cover.
