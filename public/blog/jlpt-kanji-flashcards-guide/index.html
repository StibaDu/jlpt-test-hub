How to Use Flashcards for JLPT Kanji (N5–N3) — the Spaced Way

Flashcards work — if you space them correctly. Every JLPT learner eventually meets spaced repetition (SRS), but most use it wrong: they either cram 200 kanji the week before the test or review everything daily until they burn out. Both fail.

The kanji counts, so nothing surprises you

Level Kanji Vocabulary Study time (total, from zero)
N5 ~100 ~800 300–450 h
N4 ~300 ~1,500 +450–720 h
N3 ~650 ~3,750 +900–1,320 h

That's ~650 kanji by N3. At 10 new cards/day you'll need 65 days of pure acquisition — spaced repetition exists because human memory forgets faster than that.

Why spacing works (the science in one paragraph)

Memory is not a bucket — it's a curve. Every time you recall something just as you're about to forget it, the curve flattens dramatically. Review too early = wasted time; too late = relearn from scratch. Spaced repetition puts each review right at the forgetting edge: Again (1 day), Got it (6 days), then stretching intervals of weeks as the item hardens.

The two failure modes to avoid

  1. Cram, forget, repeat — massing 200 kanji in a weekend gives you a great score on day 2 and nothing on day 200.
  2. Review everything, forever — reviewing kanji you mastered 3 months ago daily eats hours that new kanji need. SRS exists to eliminate exactly this review waste.

The habit loop that survives

  1. Same time, same place — flashcards pair best with an existing anchor (morning coffee, commute)
  2. Small daily dose — 15–30 cards beat 200 on Sunday
  3. Rate honestly — when you almost knew a kanji, rate it "didn't know." Flattering yourself defeats the algorithm
  4. Don't skip 2 days in a row — the single habit-killer (day 3 becomes day 30)

Use your actual mistakes as cards

The highest-value flashcards aren't generic decks — they're cards built from your own wrong answers. A kanji you got wrong in a real practice test carries context (which sentence tripped you, what distractor fooled you) that a random deck card lacks.
